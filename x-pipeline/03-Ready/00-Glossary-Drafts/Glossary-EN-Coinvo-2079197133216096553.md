By blending 20% ethanol into every liter of gasoline, the government replaces imported crude with domestically grown sugarcane and maize, saving billions in foreign exchange.

In this post, we’ll touch the basics of Energy Sovereignty and Fiscal Dominance to understand how one well-intended policy can lock a central bank in a trap.

Energy Sovereignty is a nation’s ability to independently secure its energy needs from domestic sources, reducing reliance on volatile global oil markets and foreign supply chains. When a country relies heavily on imported energy, its entire economy is hostage to geopolitical shocks, price swings, and supply disruptions. Achieving energy sovereignty through biofuels, renewables, or domestic fossil fuel production stabilizes the trade balance, keeps capital at home, and insulates the economy from external pressure. It also lets the government redirect spending to domestic producers instead of foreign oil exporters.

Imagine your family gets all its food from a supermarket that’s far away and sometimes closes without warning. One day, you decide to grow your own vegetables and raise chickens in your backyard. Even if your homegrown food isn’t perfect, you never go hungry when the store is empty or prices spike. That’s what energy sovereignty does for a country.

India’s E20 mandate is a textbook case. By blending 20% ethanol produced from sugarcane and maize, India cuts crude oil imports that previously covered 85% of its fuel needs, saving foreign exchange and giving farmers a stable market.

But this push for Energy Sovereignty doesn’t come free. To keep ethanol prices attractive for farmers and mills, the government pours in subsidies that swell an already stretched budget. As sovereign debt balloons, the central bank is gradually boxed in, which brings us to Fiscal Dominance.

Fiscal Dominance is a condition where a government’s borrowing needs and deficit levels become so large that they dictate monetary policy, forcing the central bank to prioritize keeping the government solvent over controlling inflation. When fiscal dominance takes hold, the central bank loses its ability to fight inflation effectively. It has to keep interest rates lower than needed, or even print money to buy government bonds, because any sharp rate hike would blow up the government’s interest costs and risk a default. This erodes the currency’s value and creates a vicious cycle of higher deficits and inflation.

Think of a friend who keeps borrowing money from you until the amount grows so large that you’re afraid to ask for it back because he’d go bankrupt and you’d lose everything. Instead, you give him even more money just to keep him afloat and stop asking for the old loans. That’s the central bank and the government under fiscal dominance.

India’s biofuel subsidies are slowly pulling the RBI into this exact dynamic. With the government unable to cut ethanol support without risking farmer protests and political backlash, fiscal deficits keep widening. The RBI is forced to tolerate higher inflation because any aggressive rate hike would explode sovereign borrowing costs and turn the debt into a crisis. The rupee weakens, import prices rise, and the very sovereignty India sought through ethanol ends up quietly transferring the cost onto every citizen through a debased currency.
