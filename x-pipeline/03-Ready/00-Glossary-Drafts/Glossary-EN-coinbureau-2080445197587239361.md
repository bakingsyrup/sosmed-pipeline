Iran just rejected the ceasefire proposal the US sent through Iraq’s Prime Minister, and oil markets didn’t wait for the actual shortage to hit — they started pricing the danger immediately. Brent crude shot past $100 a barrel in July 2026.

That surge is not about real supply loss yet; it is the Geopolitical Risk Premium in action. Every missile, every Houthi strike on a Saudi tanker in the Red Sea, makes traders demand extra compensation for holding a barrel that might become stranded next week. So you pay more at the pump today for oil that is still physically flowing.

Now, what happens when the disruption finally chokes the real flow of barrels? That’s a Supply Shock. Iran’s refusal to give up long‑term influence over the Strait of Hormuz keeps the threat alive, and whenever cargo ships are actually blocked, the bakery runs out of flour. Less oil means the price shoots even higher, and there’s no quick fix.

These two forces work in sequence. First, the premium lifts prices on fear alone — we feel it in higher logistics costs. When the shock materializes, central banks lose room to cut rates because inflation is already sticky. Suddenly, a war far away becomes a direct cost to our living expenses.

The lesson from this five‑month conflict: geopolitical headlines never stay in the news section. They print themselves into the price tag of everything we buy.
