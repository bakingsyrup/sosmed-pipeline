Michael Saylor Just Published 110 Reasons Against BIP 110

The proposal has barely 0.4% miner support, far from its own 55% lock-in requirement.

In this post, we will touch the fundamentals of Soft Fork and Consensus Rules to understand why this tiny proposal could permanently break Bitcoin's neutrality.

A Soft Fork is a backward-compatible upgrade that lets old nodes keep validating blocks and transactions without upgrading.

It's usually the least disruptive way to improve a network. But a soft fork can also quietly introduce new rules that make certain transactions invalid, even if they pay fees and follow all previous rules. The real power of a soft fork is in its subtlety: you don't split the chain, but you redefine what "valid" means for everyone going forward. That's exactly what BIP 110 tries to do by restricting data-heavy transactions.

To grasp why this is dangerous, we need to look at the foundation underneath every blockchain: Consensus Rules.

Consensus rules are the non-negotiable programmatic laws all nodes must follow to agree on a single version of the ledger. They define what makes a block valid — block size, signature checks, coin supply. They are the constitution of Bitcoin. Without them, there's no decentralized money because anyone could print coins or rewrite history. Changing these rules, even through a backward-compatible mechanism, is a governance earthquake. It shifts the boundaries of what the network considers acceptable, and if done without near-universal agreement, it breaks the social contract that gives Bitcoin its value.

What BIP 110 really threatens is the precedent of filtering transactions via consensus rules. Once we accept that a soft fork can decide one type of transaction is "spam," we've handed a censorship tool to future generations. Today it's Ordinals; tomorrow it could be privacy transactions or anything a vocal minority dislikes. That's why Saylor calls this cure more dangerous than the disease. We would be trading a temporary block space concern for a permanent crack in Bitcoin's neutral foundation — and for holders who depend on that neutrality, that's an unacceptable price. Because a chain that can be persuaded to censor is no longer sound money. It's just majority rule wearing a decentralized costume. And in that game, the minority — that's us retail — always lose.
