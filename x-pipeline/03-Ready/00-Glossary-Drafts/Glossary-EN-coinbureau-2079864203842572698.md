UK oil just breached $95 per barrel for the first time since June, and US WTI followed to over $87.

The trigger is not a refinery outage, but the collapse of the US-Iran ceasefire and rising Houthi threats to the Strait of Hormuz.

In this post, we touch the essentials of Geopolitical Risk Premium and Stagflation to understand why a few missiles can choke your portfolio.

Geopolitical Risk Premium is the extra price we pay on assets like oil simply because the market fears a future supply shock, not because crude is physically scarce yet. This premium can add dollars to a barrel weeks before any real blockade, making fuel costs jump even when supply chains are still intact. Think of it like paying extra for a flight ticket when news of a potential airport strike breaks—you pay for safety now, even though planes are still flying. In early 2026, this premium pushed Brent from $70 to $118 in a matter of weeks purely on Hormuz closure fears.

Now when this premium persists and drags energy bills higher for months, it can feed directly into Stagflation. That is a toxic economic mix where growth stalls but prices keep rising. Central banks are cornered: raising rates can cool inflation but crushes credit, while cutting rates would push prices even higher. Imagine a car stuck in mud with an overheating engine—revving to escape only makes things worse. The 1970s oil crisis showed this brutally, when an OPEC embargo gave industrial nations both recession and double-digit inflation at the same time.

So here is the loop today: a broken peace deal injects geopolitical risk premium into oil, that premium raises the cost of everything we ship and buy, and if central banks respond with tighter money to fight the inflation, a slowing economy gets pushed deeper into stagflation territory. You end up holding stocks that fall because profits evaporate, and bonds that fall because inflation eats their yield.

The wildcard? The US emergency crude reserve is at a 43-year low, so Washington has almost no oil left to calm prices. The market is naked against whatever next trigger crosses the Strait of Hormuz.
