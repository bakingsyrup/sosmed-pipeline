The Middle East Conflict Is Pushing Up Sovereign Yields

It's not just about the war itself.

It's about the Supply Shock it creates.

A Supply Shock is an unexpected event that suddenly reduces supply.

In this case, the conflict disrupted shipping in the Red Sea.

This cut the flow of oil to global markets.

Less oil available means prices go up.

Oil prices spike → inflation rises.

And here's the hidden chain reaction.

Central banks can't cut interest rates when inflation is high.

They have to hold rates steady or even raise them.

This puts pressure on Sovereign Yields.

Sovereign Yields are the interest rates governments pay on their debt.

When inflation and interest rates stay high, investors get nervous.

They demand a higher premium to hold government bonds.

So yields go up.

Higher yields mean higher borrowing costs for the government.

They have less money to spend on stimulus, subsidies, or infrastructure.

The same shock that makes oil expensive also makes debt expensive.

It's a double hit on the economy.

Governments get squeezed from both sides.

And we, as consumers and taxpayers, feel the pinch.

So next time you see a headline about a conflict, think about the Supply Shock.

And then look at the Sovereign Yields.

The two are more connected than you think.
