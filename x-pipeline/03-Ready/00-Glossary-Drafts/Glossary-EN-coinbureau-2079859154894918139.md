In this post, we’ll walk through the basics of a Market-Making Agreement and Liquidity Extraction to see how toxic deals can drain a project dry.

A Market-Making Agreement is a contract where a token issuer hires a specialized firm to continuously place buy and sell orders. The goal is to make a new token look liquid and stable, so early traders don’t slip too hard when they enter or exit. It’s a common practice and, on paper, healthy. But the compensation structure is where the poison sits. When the market maker is paid in large chunks of the token itself, its economic incentive flips from maintaining stability to offloading that free supply onto retail buyers before the price collapses.

Imagine you open a lemonade stand and hire a friend to pretend to stand in line so other kids think your drink is popular. You promise your friend a big jar of free lemonade later. Instead of just pretending to buy, your friend secretly sells that free jar to other kids at a high price before anyone tastes the lemonade. The queue vanishes, and your stand goes bust. That’s exactly how a toxic market-making agreement paves the way for liquidity extraction. Movement Labs onboarded market makers who received massive MOVE token grants; they gradually dumped into retail during the hype, collapsing the market.

This brings us directly to Liquidity Extraction.

Liquidity Extraction is the process where an insider or privileged party drains the available buying power in a market by selling large amounts of an asset to the public, leaving a dried-up order book and a crashing price. It turns regular investors into involuntary exit liquidity. When a project’s own ecosystem partners harvest short-term profit rather than defend a fair market, the entire token collapses and trust evaporates. Retail ends up holding the bag, while the extractors walk away with clean cash.

Think of a brand giving one kid 1,000 free sticker packs and telling your group they’re rare. That kid sells the packs to all of you at high prices, and once everyone owns them, the stickers are worthless and your allowance is gone. In Movement’s case, market makers used their token grants to sell into retail buy orders during the initial hype. By the time the market recognized there was no real demand, most bid liquidity had already been consumed, and late buyers were left with near-zero assets.

The pipeline is brutally simple: a toxic market-making agreement grants a party a war chest of tokens, which they then liquidate into retail through extraction mechanics. The project’s valuation evaporates, the dev company files for bankruptcy, and a few centralized actors walk away profitable while everyone else is wiped out.
