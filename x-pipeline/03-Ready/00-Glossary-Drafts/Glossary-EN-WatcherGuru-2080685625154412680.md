Apple just hit an all-time high of $4.9 trillion, briefly stealing the crown from Nvidia.

It rose 23% this year while the broader market barely moved. The rally isn't about Apple suddenly becoming the king of AI — it's about capital rotation so aggressive that fund managers are treating Apple like a bunker.

Capital rotation, the strategy of moving a chunk of your investment from one asset class to another, usually happens quietly. But lately, it's loud. Money is draining from names that have to spend billions on chips and data centers — that spending is CapEx, capital expenditure — and is pouring into companies that don't. Apple, sitting on 2.5 billion devices, can monetize AI without burning $64 billion on fabs like TSMC. That low CapEx profile keeps margins fat, and institutional investors are rewarding it with fresh cash.

Think of it like this: TSMC, Nvidia, and their peers are baking cakes in a giant oven they just bought for a fortune. Apple is the guy next door selling digital cake recipes. Customers line up, and Apple just collects the fee. That's why, when regulators suddenly open China's massive market for Apple Intelligence, the valuation jumps even higher — no new ovens required.

This rotation isn't a fluke. It's a signal. The market is finally asking: why spend billions on AI infrastructure when you can just sell the service on hardware people already own? Apple's rally says that question now has an answer — and the answer is smaller CapEx, bigger moat.
