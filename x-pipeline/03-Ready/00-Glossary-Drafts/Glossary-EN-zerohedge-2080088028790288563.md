The FDA Just Cleared Its 1,250th AI Medical Device

That’s not a typo. Regulators are now approving clinical software faster than new drugs.

With 80% of hospitals already running AI and the market barreling toward $120 billion, the real game has shifted from “can we build it” to “who is legally responsible when it fails?”

In this post, we’ll touch the basics of Clinical-grade AI and Human-in-the-loop to understand the chain that keeps us safe while hospitals automate everything behind the curtain.

When a model gets printed on a FDA clearance letter, it stops being a research project. It becomes clinical-grade AI—a tool that has passed a battery of tests proving it won’t hurt us under normal use. Without that stamp, any software in a CT scanner or a nurse’s tablet is just an uninsured liability. The clearance forces the vendor to document exactly what the AI can and cannot do, which is why a model that excels at spotting lung clots might still be banned from reading a mammogram. Think of it like a driver’s license: the state tests you on a specific vehicle class, not on “driving in general.” A clinical-grade AI holds a license for a narrow, supervised task—and that license can be revoked if it drifts.

That clearance alone isn’t enough, though. Because these models fail in ways that don’t show up on a spec sheet. They miss rare diseases. They get confused by messy scans. And that’s where Human-in-the-loop enters. HITL is a mandatory checkpoint where the AI’s output lands on a clinician’s screen with a big, blinking “confirm” button. The machine can suggest, but it cannot decide. Without this, a faulty prediction would sail straight into a patient’s chart and become a legal nightmare. The loop is what keeps the insurance premiums manageable and the hospital’s board comfortable: every automated note, every flagged tumor, every triage score has a human signature behind it. Imagine a self-driving car that never lets you take your hands off the wheel. It can brake and steer, but at the weird intersection, you’re the boss. The AI does the scanning; the doctor does the owning.

Now, this chain directly feeds the revenue model that Wall Street likes. Clinical-grade AI opens the procurement door; HITL closes the liability gap. Together, they let a hospital scale one radiologist across three times the scans without adding headcount—but they also chain that radiologist to a screen, verifying machine output instead of thinking. The silent loser is the junior pipeline that used to learn by doing. We’re building a system where the smartest doctors become full-time AI babysitters, and the entry-level roles vanish before they even get a seat. Your next scan will indeed be faster. Just know that the human who signs off on it is busier than ever, and there’s nobody left to train when they retire.
