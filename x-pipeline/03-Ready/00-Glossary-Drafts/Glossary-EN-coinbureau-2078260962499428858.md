US National Debt Hits $39.57 Trillion—Up $4.5 Trillion in Two Years

The daily run rate now exceeds $4.6 billion, and annualized interest costs have crossed $1 trillion, overtaking the entire military budget.

In this piece, we break down the fundamentals of Fiscal Dominance and Crowding Out to understand how this debt spiral silently strangles the economy.

Fiscal Dominance is a condition where a government’s borrowing needs become so overwhelming that they effectively dictate monetary policy. The central bank, in this case the Federal Reserve, can no longer act solely to control inflation. It has to consider whether a rate hike would cause the government’s interest bill to explode, pushing it toward insolvency. This means inflation is often allowed to run higher than it should, because the alternative is a debt crisis that would be far messier. What starts as a fiscal problem ends up trapping the entire economy in a cycle of ever-rising debt and depreciation.

Imagine a friend who keeps borrowing from you until his debt is so huge that asking for it back would bankrupt him and you’d lose everything. So instead, you give him more money and stop asking about the old loans. That is exactly the dynamic between a fiscally dominant government and its central bank. Right now, the U.S. is a textbook case. With over $39 trillion in debt and a deficit that shows no sign of closing, the Fed cannot raise rates enough to choke inflation without also blowing up the government’s finances.

This fiscal trap directly feeds the second mechanism: Crowding Out. Crowding Out happens when the government absorbs such a massive amount of available capital through bond issuance that private borrowers are pushed aside and forced to pay higher interest rates. The supply of loanable funds is not infinite; when the biggest borrower in the world keeps demanding more, the price of money—the interest rate—climbs for everyone else. Businesses cancel expansion plans, startups struggle to raise capital, and homebuyers face crushing mortgage rates.

To picture Crowding Out, think of a small town with a single bank. The mayor takes an enormous loan for a new town hall, leaving the bank with little left to lend. Suddenly, the baker and the mechanic must pay far higher interest, and many simply give up. That is exactly what happens across the U.S. economy when Treasury yields surge because of relentless government borrowing. In recent years, 30-year mortgage rates soared past 7% not just because of Fed policy, but because the bond market was flooded with new government debt.

Now you can see how these two forces lock together. Fiscal Dominance prevents the central bank from stopping inflation, which pushes the government to borrow even more to cover rising costs. That borrowing, in turn, triggers Crowding Out, making it more expensive for the private sector to invest and grow. The result is a slow-strangulation loop: the government gets bigger, the private sector gets weaker, and the taxpayer is left holding the bag for both higher inflation and higher borrowing costs. And all of this is unfolding without any clear exit ramp.
