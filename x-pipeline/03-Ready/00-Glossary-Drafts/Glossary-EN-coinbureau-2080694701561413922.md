Oil prices just surged past $120/bbl after a fresh wave of IRGC drone strikes targeted US bases in Bahrain and Kuwait, putting the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint back on high alert. The initial jump wasn’t because a tanker actually sank — it was the market pricing in fear, a classic Geopolitical Risk Premium at work.

That premium is the extra return buyers demand now to cover themselves against a possible supply disruption later. It’s why benchmark crude can rocket 20% in a fortnight even while physical flows remain normal, because every trader is betting the strait will eventually get blocked. Once that fear takes hold, shipping insurers raise war-risk surcharges, importers panic-buy, and governments must scramble to top up subsidy budgets.

But if the tension keeps climbing and a missile actually hits a VLCC passing Hormuz, the game shifts from what we think might happen to what just did. That’s the moment the Geopolitical Risk Premium snaps into a full‑blown Supply Shock — a sudden physical reduction in the amount of oil reaching refineries. When a critical artery like the Strait handles 20% of global oil trade and gets temporarily shut, the world instantly faces fewer barrels chasing the same daily demand.

A Supply Shock hits harder and faster than any monetary tweak. It jacks up fuel costs for logistics, factory inputs, and basic household transport — all of which feed straight into core inflation. Central banks then find their hands tied: they want to cut rates to cushion growth, but raising energy prices already stoke the CPI, leaving them boxed in.

So what starts as a line on a geopolitics newsfeed — “Iran attacks US bases in Gulf” — first shows up as a spike in your premium at the pump (Geopolitical Risk Premium), and if the strikes don’t stop, turns into a real squeeze on global oil supply that ripples through every shelf and shopping cart. That’s the causal chain from fear to shortage, and it’s why a few drones over Bahrain can end up rewriting our inflation forecasts.
