In this post, we will touch the fundamentals of Mark-to-Market and Unrealized Gain to see how they inflate a balance sheet without adding a cent of cash.

Mark-to-Market (MTM) is an accounting rule that forces a company to revalue its assets at current market prices, not at the original purchase price. Instead of showing the old $900 million cost, Alphabet had to update its books to reflect that its SpaceX stake is now worth $94.1 billion. This jump in value flows straight into the income statement as a gain — even though not a single share was sold.

Why does MTM matter? It injects volatility and can mask a company’s real operating health. For investors, a giant spike in reported profit might look fantastic, but if it’s from MTM adjustments, the company hasn’t actually received any cash to fund growth, pay debt, or return to shareholders. You have to peel back the accounting layer to see genuine performance.

Think of it like this: you buy a rare pair of sneakers for $100. A year later, everyone says they’re worth $1,000. You update your personal balance sheet to say “assets = $1,000” and you feel $900 richer. That’s MTM in action — recording the current price, not the cost. The problem? You can’t spend that $900 on rent until you sell the sneakers.

In Alphabet’s case, the SpaceX stake’s revaluation added roughly $93 billion to its equity value and boosted net income massively. That MTM gain became headline news, but the cash didn’t flow into the company’s bank account, and the shares are locked up until at least late 2026.

Now, what exactly is that paper profit? It’s called an Unrealized Gain — a profit that exists on paper because the asset’s market price rose, but ownership hasn’t been converted into cash through a sale. In accounting, it sits as a separate line item, inflating shareholder equity while the company’s actual liquidity stays unchanged.

Unrealized gains are essential to understand because they can deceive. A firm like Alphabet can report record net income, but if it’s built almost entirely on unrealized gains, operational resilience may be far weaker than the headline suggests. That’s why analysts look at adjusted earnings — removing paper profits to see the core engine. In Q2 2026, Alphabet’s adjusted EPS was just $2.85, a stark contrast to the $9.11 GAAP figure that included the SpaceX boost.

A simple analogy: your parents bought a house for $100,000 decades ago, and now it could sell for $300,000. Until they actually sign the contract and receive the cash, that $200,000 difference is unrealized — a comforting number but not spendable on groceries. Similarly, Alphabet’s $99 billion in equity gains sounds wealthy, yet it can’t pay salaries or build data centers with that locked-up value.

The SpaceX case perfectly illustrates the concept. The massive $94 billion mark was pure revaluation; the profit was unrealized. Even Google’s AI compute deal — paying SpaceX $920 million a month — doesn’t release that gain. The circularity (spending to boost SpaceX’s revenue, which supports its stock price, which inflates Alphabet’s paper value) is clever, but the cash remains distinct from the book gain.

So here’s the loop: MTM forces Alphabet to recognize the SpaceX stake at its sky-high market value, creating an enormous unrealized gain. That gain pumps net income, but investors who dig deeper see that fundamental earnings are slim. The lesson? When you see a shocking profit spike, ask whether it’s cash in the bank or just a mark-to-market illusion that can vanish when the lockup ends and the stock corrects.
