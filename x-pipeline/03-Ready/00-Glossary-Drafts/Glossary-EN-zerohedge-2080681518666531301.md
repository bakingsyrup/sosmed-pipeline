An Exchange Stabilization Fund is essentially a Treasury’s war chest for currency crisis. Unlike an IMF program, it doesn’t come with a hundred policy conditionalities attached — you get the dollars quickly, and the provider gets a direct say in your next moves. For Pakistan, sitting on thin reserves and a $7 billion IMF program, a $10 billion ESF tap would be a financial lifeline that also reduces its reliance on Saudi and Chinese goodwill. But that $10 billion request opens the door to a much stickier trap.

When a government’s debt pile becomes the central organiser of all economic policy, you enter a state called Fiscal Dominance. Here, the central bank stops fighting inflation properly because any serious rate hike would explode the government’s interest bill and trigger a default. Instead, it keeps funding the state — quietly printing money or keeping rates artificially low — and inflation is allowed to run hotter for longer. Imagine a friend who owes you so much that you stop asking for repayment and start lending him even more just so he doesn’t collapse. That’s the central bank trapped under fiscal dominance, and the US itself is already living it with a $39 trillion debt load and $1 trillion‑plus annual interest costs.

Pakistan is trying to trade its geopolitical position for an Exchange Stabilization Fund buffer. But if the US says no, Islamabad is left with the IMF demanding further austerity, Saudi Arabia questioning its loyalty, and China watching its trade routes remain blocked anyway. At that point, the country doesn’t just lose a diplomatic initiative — it drifts deeper into its own fiscal dominance, where every cent of funding is consumed by debt service and the currency weakens no matter what the central bank does.

The real story here isn’t mediation. It’s a country trying to write a $10 billion insurance policy against its own fiscal death spiral.
