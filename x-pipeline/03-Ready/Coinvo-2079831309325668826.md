### 1. Verification Status
**True.** As of July 2026, both Poland and Hungary have implemented policies to restrict foreign labor, driven by domestic political pressure and nationalist sentiment. 

*   **Hungary:** Effective June 6, 2026, the government suspended new applications for "guest worker residence permits" by withdrawing the list of eligible third countries. While other employment-based residence permits still exist, they are significantly more restrictive and time-consuming.
*   **Poland:** The government has been actively tightening its labor market. Polish authorities have significantly reduced the issuance of work permits for non-EU citizens (a 22% reduction reported for the previous year) and implemented stricter regulations on foreign employment to address nationalist concerns.

### 2. Key Facts & Numbers
*   **Hungary’s Policy:** Government Decree No. 92/2026 (enacted June 5, 2026) halted new guest worker residence permit applications for nationals of countries including the Philippines, Georgia, and Armenia. 
*   **Poland’s Trend:** Poland issued eight times fewer work visas in Q1 2026 compared to the same period in 2022. By the end of 2025, Poland had a record 1.29 million foreign workers (approx. 8% increase year-over-year), with Ukrainians making up the largest share (~857,000).
*   **Economic Contribution:** A 2026 report (Deloitte, Ipsos, Institute of Public Affairs) estimated that non-EU workers contributed up to 10.7% of Poland's economic output in 2025.

### 3. Comparative Baseline Data
*   **Labor Market Reliance:** Both countries face severe demographic challenges (aging populations and low birth rates). Poland’s labor force relies on over 1 million foreign workers; Hungary’s reliance is lower (approx. 2% of the workforce), but specific sectors like manufacturing, logistics, and hospitality are heavily dependent on these migrant pools.
*   **Public Sentiment:** In Poland, ~66% of respondents in a January 2026 IBRiS survey favored accepting fewer non-EU migrants. In Hungary, nearly 50% of the population opposes migration from poorer countries.

### 4. Macro/Geopolitical Context
These moves represent a pivot by "centrist" ruling parties—Donald Tusk’s government in Poland and Peter Magyar’s government in Hungary (who took power in April 2026)—to neutralize criticism from their right-wing nationalist rivals. By curbing immigration, these leaders aim to appease domestic voters while navigating a difficult economic trade-off: maintaining growth in an environment where the domestic labor supply is shrinking.

### 5. Most Affected Party & Impact
*   **Businesses:** Manufacturing, hospitality, logistics, and construction firms are the primary losers. They face immediate staffing shortages, potential project delays (e.g., reports of factory expansions being halted), and increased labor costs as they are forced to compete for a smaller, domestic-only labor pool.
*   **Governments:** They gain short-term political stability and "nationalist" credibility but risk long-term economic stagnation, lower tax revenues, and increased pressure on pension/healthcare systems due to the aging domestic population.
*   **Financial Flow:** The flow of foreign remittances out of these countries will likely decrease as the number of foreign workers is capped or reduced.

### 6. Secondary/Hidden Effects
*   **Wage Inflation:** As the labor supply tightens, businesses may be forced to raise wages to attract local workers, which could exacerbate inflationary pressures.
*   **Capital Flight/Investment Risk:** Foreign investors—particularly those in manufacturing—may reconsider long-term capital investments or factory expansions if they cannot guarantee a stable, affordable workforce.
*   **Regulatory Friction:** As these countries fail to align with broader EU labor migration directives (e.g., the Single Permit Directive), they may face increased legal and diplomatic friction with the European Commission.
