# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### 1. Verification Status
**True.** The Pentagon, through the U.S. Army and Air Force, is actively pursuing a strategy to lease "underutilized but non-excess" military land to commercial developers to build hyperscale AI data centers. The program operates under the **Enhanced Use Lease (EUL)** framework, driven by a Trump administration executive order.
*   **Status:** The Army has conditionally selected developers for two initial sites: **Fort Bliss, Texas** (Carlyle Group) and **Dugway Proving Ground, Utah** (CyrusOne, backed by KKR and BlackRock). These are the first two "real" deals, with at least a dozen other installations across the U.S. being opened for proposals.

### 2. Key Facts & Numbers
*   **Projected Cost:** Each hyperscale data center is estimated to cost approximately **$2 billion**.
*   **Land Scale:** The Fort Bliss site involves roughly **1,384 acres**; the Dugway Proving Ground site involves roughly **1,201 acres**.
*   **Financial Flow:** The military is not receiving traditional cash rent. Instead, it is receiving **"in-kind consideration,"** specifically guaranteed access to a portion of the data center's computing power to support military and warfighting needs.
*   **Timeline:** Initial operating capability is projected for **fiscal year 2027** at Fort Bliss and **2029** at Dugway.
*   **Total Scope:** At least a dozen military installations have been identified or opened for potential commercial data center development.

### 3. Comparative Baseline Data
*   **Historical Context:** While the military has used Enhanced Use Leases (EULs) for decades (e.g., solar farms, business parks), those projects were generally smaller, complemented existing military missions, and were designed to generate revenue or energy for the base. The current AI data center initiative is a **sharp departure** because these facilities are primarily commercial and are not explicitly designed to serve the Department of Defense (DoD), despite the "in-kind" compute arrangement.

### 4. Historical Precedents & Past Benchmarks
*   **Category A (Historical Event Anchor):** The use of 10 U.S.C. § 2667 (EUL authority) is a long-standing legal tool. Past successes include renewable energy projects (e.g., solar farms at Edwards AFB) and research labs (Kirtland AFB). These were typically smaller in scale and had lower energy/water footprints compared to the massive power requirements (100+ megawatts) of modern hyperscale AI data centers.
*   **Category B (Parallel Industry Playbook):** The "Land-for-Compute" model mirrors the "Infrastructure-as-a-Service" (IaaS) adoption curve where the government acts as an anchor tenant to de-risk private capital investment in exchange for guaranteed service priority.
*   **Category C (Entity Track-Record Anchor):** The DoD has previously partnered with major cloud providers (AWS, Azure) for information infrastructure. However, those were service-purchase agreements. This new model shifts the DoD into a **landlord role** for commercial-scale infrastructure, introducing new risks regarding data sovereignty and physical security.

### 5. Macro/Geopolitical Context
The initiative is a response to the "AI arms race," where the U.S. seeks to maintain technological superiority over adversaries. By leveraging federal land, the DoD aims to bypass local land-use constraints (NIMBYism) and power-grid bottlenecks that typically slow down private sector data center construction.

### 6. Most Affected Party & Impact
*   **Gains:** Private developers (Carlyle, CyrusOne/KKR/BlackRock) gain access to secure, massive tracts of land, often bypassing lengthy local zoning and environmental hurdles. The military gains high-performance computing power without upfront capital expenditure.
*   **Loses/Affected:** Local communities near these bases face significant strain on regional water and electricity grids. Congress and local stakeholders are expressing concern over the potential for these sites to become "kinetic" targets in a conflict, as well as the environmental impact of massive water and energy consumption.

### 7. Secondary/Hidden Effects
*   **Grid Isolation:** To mitigate local grid strain, some proposals mandate that these data centers generate their own power and not connect to local grids, potentially creating "islanded" energy hubs that could be repurposed for military microgrid resiliency.
*   **Dual-Use Conflict:** Because these are "commercial" facilities that can sell excess capacity, there is a risk of intellectual property and national security data being co-located with commercial traffic, complicating security protocols.
