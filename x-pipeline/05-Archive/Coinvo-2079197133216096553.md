# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### Verification Status: **Partially True / Contextually Nuanced**
The claim that India requires its gas stations to sell E20 (20% ethanol, 80% petrol) is **true**. However, the characterization of E20 as "impure fuel" is a subjective, non-technical term. While it is scientifically accurate that ethanol has lower energy density than pure gasoline—which can lead to a slight reduction in fuel efficiency (typically 3–5%)—the implication that it is "impure" is misleading, as it is a regulated, government-mandated fuel designed for environmental and economic objectives.

### Key Facts & Numbers
*   **Mandate**: India has moved to a nationwide rollout of E20 fuel as part of its Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) programme, with the goal of reaching 20% blending by the 2025–26 Ethanol Supply Year (ESY).
*   **Efficiency Impact**: The Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas has acknowledged a potential **3–5% reduction in fuel efficiency** in some vehicles.
*   **Economic Rationale**: India imports over 85% of its crude oil. The EBP programme aims to reduce this import dependency, save billions in foreign exchange, and provide income to the domestic agricultural sector (sugarcane/maize farmers).
*   **Vehicle Compatibility**: Vehicles manufactured with E20-compatible materials (post-2023/2025) are designed to handle the blend. Issues are most frequently reported by owners of older vehicles not specifically tuned for higher ethanol concentrations.

### Comparative Baseline Data
*   **Blending Progress**: India's blending rate has steadily increased: ~8.1% in 2020–21, 10% in 2021–22, 12.1% in 2022–23, and 14.6% in 2023–24.
*   **Capacity**: India increased its ethanol production capacity from 700 crore litres to 1,500 crore litres as part of its roadmap.

### Macro/Geopolitical Context
The shift is a strategic move to achieve **Energy Sovereignty** and reduce the trade deficit caused by high oil imports. It aligns with global trends in biofuel adoption to lower carbon emissions, though India's rapid transition has faced pushback due to a lack of public consultation and transparency regarding long-term impacts on older vehicle fleets.

### Most Affected Party & Impact
*   **Winners**: **Agricultural sector (farmers/sugar mills)** and **Government/Public Sector Oil Marketing Companies**, who benefit from domestic production and reduced import bills.
*   **Losers/Affected**: **Owners of older, non-compliant vehicles** who may face reduced mileage, potential corrosion of rubber/plastic fuel system components, and warranty disputes.
*   **Financial Flow**: Consumers pay the same (or sometimes higher) prices for fuel with lower energy density, while the government directs capital toward domestic biofuel infrastructure.

### Secondary/Hidden Effects
*   **Legal/Consumer Liability**: A surge in consumer disputes is occurring, with some courts ruling in favor of consumers whose vehicles were allegedly damaged by E20, forcing manufacturers to address warranty and compatibility claims.
*   **Maintenance Costs**: Mechanics report an increase in issues related to clogged fuel injectors and degraded seals in older cars, potentially increasing long-term maintenance costs for lower-income vehicle owners.
