# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### 1. Verification Status
**True.** Multiple reliable reports from July 20–22, 2026, confirm that shipping companies, notably the Sinokor Group, are offering significant financial incentives to crews willing to transit the Strait of Hormuz. This is occurring amid an ongoing conflict between the U.S./Israel and Iran, which has severely disrupted maritime traffic in the region.

### 2. Key Facts & Numbers
*   **The Incentive:** Sinokor Group, the world's largest owner of supertankers, has offered crews **six months of extra salary** for a single round-trip voyage through the Strait of Hormuz (loading in Saudi Arabia or Iraq, unloading in the Gulf of Oman).
*   **Duration/Scope:** The voyage is estimated to take approximately one month.
*   **Baseline Pay:** On oil tankers, captains earn up to **$15,000/month**, while junior sailors (ratings) typically earn around **$1,500/month** in normal conditions.
*   **Casualties:** Since the conflict began on February 28, 2026, at least **59 commercial ships** have been attacked in the region, resulting in the deaths of **17 seafarers**.
*   **Traffic Impact:** Traffic through the Strait remains "heavily suppressed," with only 30 verified crossings logged between July 17 and July 19, 2026. Total traffic is down approximately 90% year-on-year.

### 3. Comparative Baseline Data
*   **Previous Status:** Prior to the current escalation (which saw a collapse of a brief détente/Memorandum of Understanding in mid-July), traffic had seen a temporary resurgence.
*   **Historical Context:** Before the February 2026 conflict, the Strait of Hormuz accounted for approximately 25% of the world’s seaborne oil trade and 20% of its LNG.
*   **Shift in Tactics:** A significant portion (nearly 70%) of current tanker transits are now conducted "dark" (with AIS tracking signals disabled) to evade detection and targeting.

### 4. Macro/Geopolitical Context
The Strait of Hormuz has become a central theater in the U.S.–Iran war. The U.S. has engaged in a sustained campaign of strikes against Iranian infrastructure (including bridges and power plants) in retaliation for Iranian attacks on merchant shipping. The shipping industry is currently caught in the middle of these "tit-for-tat" strikes, with insurance premiums soaring and many owners refusing to send vessels into the zone at all, leading to the "danger money" bonuses as a desperate measure to maintain supply chains.

### 5. Most Affected Party & Impact
*   **Seafarers:** They face the highest direct risk—the choice between significant financial gain (often equivalent to half a year's wages) and potential death. Many are reportedly refusing the bonuses and leaving their vessels.
*   **Shipping Companies (e.g., Sinokor):** These firms gain massive premiums for successful transits but face extreme operational risk and potential loss of assets.
*   **Global Energy Markets:** The disruption has contributed to a surge in oil prices (Brent crude recently trading near $95/barrel).
*   **Governments:** The U.S. and its allies are struggling to maintain the flow of energy while Iran uses the threat of closure as leverage in the broader conflict.

### 6. Secondary/Hidden Effects
*   **Shadow Fleet Expansion:** The crisis is accelerating the use of non-sanctioned, "shadow fleet" vessels that operate without standard safety or insurance protocols, increasing the risk of environmental disasters (oil spills) in the event of an attack.
*   **Labor Shortages:** The high-risk environment is creating a long-term retention crisis for qualified maritime labor, as the pool of sailors willing to accept these "danger zone" contracts shrinks.
*   **Strategic Reprioritization:** The blockade is forcing regional powers (like Saudi Arabia and the UAE) to look for alternative, non-maritime, or non-Hormuz export routes, which could permanently alter regional trade dependencies.
