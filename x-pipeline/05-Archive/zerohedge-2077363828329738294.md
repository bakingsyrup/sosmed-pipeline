# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### 1. Verification Status
**True.** Federal banking regulators (including the OCC, FDIC, and NCUA) and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) have issued guidance directing financial institutions to heighten scrutiny of credit risks associated with borrowers who lack legal work authorization in the U.S.. This follows a May 2026 Executive Order from the Trump administration aimed at "Restoring Integrity to America's Financial System".

### 2. Key Facts & Numbers
*   **Regulatory Action:** Guidance issued in June and July 2026 reminds banks of their existing obligations under the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Regulation Z to assess a borrower’s "ability to repay".
*   **Risk Factors:** Regulators explicitly noted that lending to individuals without legal work authorization may present "elevated credit risk" because the risk of removal from the U.S. could disrupt the borrower's income.
*   **FinCEN Advisory:** The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) issued an advisory including 18 "red flag" indicators for banks to detect potential payroll tax fraud, identity theft, and suspicious activity linked to the unlawful employment of unauthorized workers.
*   **Scope:** The guidance does not formally ban lending to these individuals but strongly encourages a risk-based approach, effectively signaling that banks should account for immigration status as a material financial risk factor.

### 3. Comparative Baseline Data
*   **Prior Policy:** During the Biden administration (specifically around 2023), the CFPB and DOJ had issued a joint statement that cautioned lenders against using immigration status in underwriting, arguing it could lead to discrimination under the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA).
*   **Shift:** In January 2026, the CFPB and DOJ withdrew that 2023 statement, clarifying that creditors are permitted to consider immigration/citizenship status when it is "necessary to ascertain the creditor's rights and remedies regarding repayment". The current 2026 guidance builds on this reversal, moving from "permissive" to "heightened scrutiny".

### 4. Macro/Geopolitical Context
The administration is framing the presence of unauthorized workers as a "systemic risk" to the financial system. This is part of a broader policy push to link immigration enforcement with financial oversight, using the banking sector as a tool for immigration control. This mirrors other administration efforts to restrict access to government benefits and services for unauthorized residents.

### 5. Most Affected Party & Impact
*   **Financial Institutions (Banks/Credit Unions):** They face increased compliance costs and the need to update underwriting models to incorporate "immigration status" as a risk variable. They are now expected to be more vigilant in "Know Your Customer" (KYC) processes.
*   **Unauthorized Borrowers:** This population faces higher barriers to obtaining credit cards, mortgages, and other loans, as lenders may now view their lack of work authorization as a primary indicator of default risk.
*   **Financial Flow:** The flow of credit to unauthorized residents is likely to tighten, potentially pushing these individuals toward alternative, less-regulated financial services (shadow banking) or cash-based transactions.

### 6. Secondary/Hidden Effects
*   **Shadow Banking Growth:** As traditional, regulated banks pull back from these borrowers to avoid regulatory scrutiny, demand for high-interest, predatory, or informal lending may rise.
*   **Data Aggregation:** Banks may increasingly rely on third-party verification services to screen for immigration status, creating a new market for "immigration risk assessment" data.
*   **Compliance Burden on Employers:** Because banks are being asked to report suspicious activity regarding the "unlawful employment" of unauthorized workers, the banking sector is effectively being deputized to perform employment verification, which could lead to increased scrutiny of small business payrolls.
