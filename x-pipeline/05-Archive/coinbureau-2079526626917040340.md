# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### **Verification Status: TRUE**
The report is accurate based on current news as of July 21, 2026. The Trump administration is preparing to replace a temporary 10% global tariff (set to expire on July 24, 2026) with a new, more permanent tariff structure targeting approximately 60 countries. These new duties are expected to range from 10% to 12.5% and are being justified through Section 301 investigations into forced labor practices and excess manufacturing capacity, rather than the emergency powers previously struck down by the Supreme Court.

### **Key Facts & Numbers**
*   **Expiration Date:** The current temporary 10% global tariff, enacted under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, expires on **July 24, 2026**.
*   **New Tariff Rates:** The proposed structure involves a two-tier system: **10%** for some trading partners and **12.5%** for others, depending on the findings of the Section 301 investigations.
*   **Scope:** The measures target approximately **60 countries**.
*   **Legal Shift:** The administration is moving away from the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which the Supreme Court ruled illegal in February 2026 (case: *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*), toward Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 to provide a more durable legal basis.
*   **Refunds:** The U.S. government has been required to refund approximately **$81 billion** in tariffs collected under the now-illegal IEEPA framework during the current fiscal year.

### **Comparative Baseline Data**
*   **Average Tariff Rate:** In early 2025, the average effective U.S. tariff rate was approximately 2.5%, which rose to 27% by April 2025. As of July 2026, the average effective rate stands at approximately **12%**.
*   **Market Context:** The shift to Section 301 investigations is a strategic pivot to bypass the 150-day limit inherent in the Section 122 powers used for the expiring temporary tariffs.

### **Macro/Geopolitical Context**
The Trump administration is attempting to maintain a protectionist trade wall despite judicial pushback. By utilizing Section 301—which allows for retaliation against "unfair trade practices"—the administration is attempting to embed these tariffs into long-term trade policy, potentially complicating relationships with major allies (EU, Japan, South Korea) and partners (Canada, Mexico) ahead of the November 2026 midterm elections.

### **Most Affected Party & Impact**
*   **Losers:**
    *   **Importers/Businesses:** Must navigate higher input costs and legal uncertainty regarding tariff compliance and potential retroactive disputes.
    *   **Consumers:** Continued reliance on tariffs generally keeps the cost of imported goods high, contributing to inflationary pressure.
    *   **Foreign Exporters:** Countries (such as Japan, South Korea, and EU members) face sustained or increased barriers to the U.S. market.
*   **Gains:**
    *   **Federal Treasury (Attempted):** The administration seeks to replace the revenue lost from the Supreme Court-mandated refunds.
    *   **Domestic Manufacturers:** Protected sectors (metals, autos, etc.) face less competition from foreign imports, though this is often offset by higher costs for downstream manufacturing inputs.

### **Secondary/Hidden Effects**
*   **Legal & Regulatory Fatigue:** The constant shifting of legal justifications (IEEPA to Section 122 to Section 301) creates massive compliance costs for U.S. businesses and customs brokers.
*   **Retaliation Risk:** Trading partners are increasingly developing their own legal frameworks to prosecute or sanction U.S. companies in response to these tariffs (e.g., China’s new laws targeting foreign organizations).
*   **Political Constraints:** With midterm elections approaching, the administration is facing pressure from voters concerned about the cost of living, which may limit the aggressiveness of future tariff hikes despite the administration's stated goals.
