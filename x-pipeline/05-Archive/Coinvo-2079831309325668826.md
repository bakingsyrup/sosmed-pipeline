# Fact-Checking & Research Brief
### **Verification Status: TRUE**
The claim is **accurate**. Both Poland and Hungary have implemented significant restrictions on foreign labor since June 2026, driven by a combination of nationalist political pressure and a desire to regulate migration flows. Business leaders in both countries have publicly warned that these measures threaten operational capacity, with some explicitly stating that their sectors "cannot currently function" without foreign labor.

---

### **Key Facts & Numbers**
*   **Hungary**: As of June 6, 2026, the government suspended new "guest worker" residence permit applications by removing all third-country eligibility from the list. This primarily impacts recruitment from countries like the Philippines, Georgia, and Armenia.
*   **Poland**: New regulations enforced in June 2026 have tightened labor market access for non-EU citizens. The Polish government previously slashed work permits for non-EU citizens by 22% in 2025.
*   **Economic Impact**: A 2026 report (Deloitte, Ipsos, Institute of Public Affairs) indicates that non-EU workers contributed up to **10.7% of Poland’s economic output** in 2025.
*   **Business Sentiment**: In both countries, representatives from the hospitality and manufacturing sectors have warned that labor shortages are so severe that they may be forced to cut shifts or halt planned expansions.

---

### **Comparative Baseline Data**
*   **Demographics**: Both nations face historic labor shortages; Poland’s birth rates are at their lowest since World War II. The Polish Economic Institute (PIE) previously forecast a potential loss of **2.1 million workers by 2035**, which could reduce GDP by 6%–8% in the industrial sector alone.
*   **Visa Issuance**: Poland reported issuing eight times fewer work visas in Q1 2026 compared to Q1 2022 (partially due to rule changes regarding Ukrainians).
*   **Workforce Reliance**: In Hungary, while foreign workers officially account for only ~1–2% of the total workforce, their concentration in specific sectors like manufacturing and hospitality is as high as **20%**.

---

### **Macro/Geopolitical Context**
*   **Political Pivot**: The restrictions follow electoral and political pressure. In Hungary, the new government (Tisza Party) won in April 2026 on a platform promising to prioritize domestic workers. In Poland, the Tusk government is navigating a "balancing act" to appease voters who support stricter immigration policies while maintaining the labor force required for industrial growth.
*   **EU Compliance**: Both countries are currently under pressure from the European Commission (as of July 2026) regarding the transposition of EU directives on labor migration, which are intended to *facilitate* rather than restrict the admission of non-EU workers.

---

### **Most Affected Party & Impact**
*   **Most Affected**: **Manufacturing, hospitality, and logistics businesses** are the primary losers. These industries rely heavily on the "guest worker" permit category for low-to-medium-skilled labor.
*   **Financial Flow**: The restriction acts as an artificial supply shock to the labor market. Businesses face higher wage costs (to attract domestic workers who may not be available) or direct output losses (shutting down shifts). Governments may see a short-term boost in public support but risk a long-term erosion of tax revenue and GDP growth as industries struggle to fill roles.

---

### **Secondary/Hidden Effects**
*   **Operational Shifts**: Companies may accelerate automation or shift production to other EU countries with more lenient labor policies to maintain competitiveness.
*   **Contractual Defaults**: Polish service providers operating across the EU are reportedly struggling to fulfill cross-border contracts because their foreign employees cannot be easily moved or legalized for work, leading to lost market share to competitors in other EU states.
*   **"Aging Before Rich":** Economists warn that these policies exacerbate the "aging before rich" trap, where the tax base shrinks before the country has developed the high-productivity, high-tech economy needed to support an elderly population.
